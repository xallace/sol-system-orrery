Model notes

About this orrery

What you are seeing

The scene is a geometric map of the Sun and planets at the selected instant—not a view from Earth. Positions are heliocentric and expressed in the J2000 mean ecliptic frame: +x toward the J2000 equinox, +y 90° eastward in the ecliptic plane, and +z toward ecliptic north. No light-time, aberration, atmospheric refraction, or observer location is applied.

Orbital distances use one consistent linear scale. The visible discs use a separate, uniform ×12 exaggeration so that planets do not vanish; the Sun is likewise enlarged. Labels and minimum-size markers are navigation aids, not physical sizes.

Ephemeris & time

Planet and Moon vectors come from Astronomy Engine 2.1.19 by Don Cross (MIT). It is based on the VSOP87 model, with the author reporting agreement within ±1 arcminute against NOVAS and testing against JPL Horizons. This explorer uses HelioVector and GeoMoon, rotates the J2000 equatorial vectors into the J2000 ecliptic with the library's Rotation_EQJ_ECL matrix, and fits no curves of its own.

Civil timestamps are handled in UTC. The browser's local time zone is used only for entry and display. Leap years follow the proleptic Gregorian calendar built into JavaScript's Date. Astronomy Engine converts UTC to Terrestrial Time (TT) internally using the Espenak–Meeus ΔT model; the bottom status line displays the applied ΔT in seconds. The app does not invent leap seconds. The app deliberately restricts input to 1900-01-01 through 2100-12-31 UTC.

Precision is not accuracy

Numbers may be printed to many decimals, but that says nothing about physical accuracy. Position accuracy is limited by the ephemeris model, its epoch and truncation, ΔT uncertainty, and (for the Moon) short-term dynamics. “Precision” means repeatable numerical detail; “accuracy” means closeness to reality. Treat close-up planet views as visualizations, not navigation data.

Independent check against JPL Horizons DE441 geometric vectors (J2000 ecliptic): 2025-01-01 — Earth 877 km (1.2″), Mars 1,634 km (1.4″), Neptune 338,282 km (15.6″); 2035-07-04 — Earth 1,175 km (1.6″), Mars 1,330 km (1.3″), Neptune 158,397 km (7.3″). Full method and caveats are in the README.

Orbit curves

Each orbit path is sampled from the same ephemeris at hundreds of instants across one sidereal period, then drawn as a polyline. Curves therefore agree with the position model rather than coming from a separate circular orbit. A polyline is a visual approximation only in its segment density. Curves are refreshed as time moves so that secular changes are represented. Near 1900 or 2100 a curve may be sampled slightly outside the selectable interval so the loop still closes; body positions never are.

What is illustrative

Planet discs use generated, non-cartographic textures and approximate colours. They are not surface maps. Saturn's ring plane is oriented from Astronomy Engine's IAU rotation-axis model; the ring spacing, opacity, and texture are illustrative, and the spin phase is not modeled. Other axial tilts, rotation phases, surface features, clouds, and magnetic fields are not modeled. The star field is decorative and does not represent a sky catalogue. Sizes are exaggerated as described above.

Known limitations

  • No moons other than Earth's Moon; no asteroids, comets, or Pluto.
  • The Moon's geocentric vector is modeled, but its orbit curve is drawn relative to Earth and is not a precisely closed ellipse.
  • VSOP87 is an approximation to the full JPL DE ephemerides. The small differences are visible in the README validation table.
  • Browser local-time input around a daylight-saving transition can be ambiguous. The UTC mode is unambiguous. In Local mode the exact UTC endpoints can also carry a local date just across midnight.
  • The camera and all assets are local; no live position service or API key is used.
